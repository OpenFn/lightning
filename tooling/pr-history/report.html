What our reviewers
actually ask for

Four and a half years of review feedback, read in aggregate for the first time. The categories were induced from the corpus rather than guessed at, and the corpus disagreed with the guess.

2,235 pull requests 4,312 reviews 4,282 units of human feedback 2022-02-09 to 2026-09-02
00

Five things worth knowing

Ranked by how much they should change what we do, not by how surprising they are.

  1. F1Formal change requests are mostly acceptance testing, not code critique. The largest identifiable register in CHANGES_REQUESTED bodies is a reviewer running the feature and reporting what they saw. Code craft feedback lives somewhere else entirely.
  2. F2The asks people comply with silently are exactly the ones a machine could make. Changelog and missing-test asks draw a reply 18–19% of the time against a 43% baseline. Naming, abstraction and diff-hygiene asks draw one 62–85% of the time.
  3. F3Review coverage regressed sharply in 2026. Merged-without-any-review held at 11–17% through 2024 and 2025, then roughly doubled to 30% in Q2 and 33% in Q3. Latency moved the same way.
  4. F4PR size predicts how hard review will be, but not whether it happens. An XL PR draws a change request seven times more often than an XS one, yet is no more likely to be reviewed at all.
  5. F5Three reviewers carry 62% of all review. Twenty-two people have reviewed something; five account for 80%.
This measures process, not people. Review counts track team composition, role and tenure in each period. Nothing here supports a judgement about anyone's diligence, and it should not be used in a performance conversation.
01

How these categories were arrived at

This decides how much to trust everything after it, so it goes near the top rather than in an appendix.

The first version of this analysis pre-specified about fifteen plausible categories: tests, naming, error handling, Ecto, authorization, performance. It was abandoned, because counting matches against a list you wrote in advance only measures how good your guess was. The frame decides the finding.

What replaced it induces structure from the corpus three ways. Vocabulary frequency across all 4,282 units. A log-odds ratio with an informative Dirichlet prior contrasting structural slices against each other, reported as a z-score, so what comes back is what is distinctive rather than what is merely common. And term co-occurrence clustering, so groups form from how reviewers pair concepts. Then a manual read of ~110 units from a sample stratified by structure (feedback kind, review state, length, year) and deliberately not by topic, since topic was the thing being discovered.

The categories were written down afterwards, as an output. Every count below is a lower bound: a reviewer writing “this'll blow up on nil” is making a failure-mode request no pattern catches. Comments are multi-label, so shares do not sum to 100%.

F1

Two registers, and they barely share a vocabulary

Contrast change-request bodies against approvals, then inline comments against conversation, and two different activities fall out of the same dataset.

Change-request bodies
Terms most distinctive of CHANGES_REQUESTED vs APPROVED. This is the vocabulary of someone clicking through a feature.
Inline code comments
Terms most distinctive of inline comments vs PR conversation. This is the vocabulary of someone reading code.

Reading them confirms what the numbers imply. Change-request bodies are full of observed behaviour:

I'm getting the following if I attempt to change my email but don't get my current password right…

When you enter the security tab URL in the browser it renders this blank page.

I found this UI surprisingly difficult to understand at first, even knowing the platform well.

Approvals, by contrast, are almost pure social language: the terms most distinctive of them are nicely, works, tested, happy, perfect, lgtm, clean, tiny, tweaks.

One corroboration worth noting, because it was not something the text analysis was looking for. Of the twenty-two reviewers, two have never opened a pull request. One of them submitted 78 reviews and 11 change requests while writing only four inline comments. That is a person reviewing by using the product, and it is exactly the shape F1 predicts.

03
F2

Compliance and contest are cleanly separated

Whether a comment draws a reply turns out to be a good proxy for whether the ask was contested. Sorting categories against the baseline splits them almost perfectly into rule-shaped and judgement-shaped.

This is the most useful result here. The feedback people accept without a word is mechanical and rule-shaped. The feedback they argue about is judgement about design and data modelling, which is what senior review time is actually for. Right now both are delivered the same way: by a person, after the work is finished.

04
F3

Review coverage regressed in 2026

27% of the 2,151 human-authored PRs received no review at all. The headline number matters less than the trend, and the trend needed a check before it could be trusted.

Why this counts only merged PRs. A recent PR might simply not have been reviewed yet, which would manufacture a fake regression out of nothing. A merged PR that went unreviewed is settled: it will never be reviewed. That series is censoring-free, so it is the one plotted.

The changelog ask is fully mechanical and completely unautomated

It appears 29 times in change requests across all five years. The PR template already carries “I have updated the changelog” as a checkbox, and there is no CI check behind it. One review comment in the corpus reads, in full:

Please update the changelog, you marked it as done on the checklist.

A job that fails when a PR touches lib/ without touching CHANGELOG.md would end this category, and its 18% reply rate says nobody would argue about it.

Data modelling is the largest inline category and has no guideline

157 inline comments and 35 change requests concern Ecto schemas, migration structure and query shape, more than any other category, and nothing is written down about any of it. The specific asks recur: collapse several migrations touching one table into one; don't do inserts one at a time inside something shaped like a batch; move validation inside the Multi so check-and-insert share a transaction. That last one caught a real race condition. This is the highest-value thing to write down, and the source material is already in the corpus.

Acceptance findings are not a documentation gap

17% of change requests are a reviewer discovering behaviour by using the feature. More prose will not fix that. Either the author exercises their own change before requesting review, or the E2E suite covers the path. The E2E guidance already exists in four files, so the gap is in what gets written, not in knowing how to write it.

08

What I'd actually do

In order of return on effort.

  1. Add a changelog CI check. Smallest change with a guaranteed effect, and the data says it will not be resisted.
  2. Write the missing Ecto and migrations guideline. Largest category, zero coverage, and the recurring asks are specific enough to write today.
  3. Work out what happened to review coverage in 2026. Merged-without-review doubled and latency rose together. That is a capacity or routing question, and it is the finding most likely to matter beyond code review.
  4. Decide deliberately whether large PRs get guaranteed review. Right now an XL PR is as likely to go unreviewed as a one-line one, while costing three times the rounds when it is reviewed.
  5. Leave abstraction pushback and naming alone. They are 62–69% contested because they are genuine judgement, and they read as a healthy norm. The only thing worth changing is when they arrive: on the approach, rather than after the implementation.
09

What this cannot tell you

  • Nothing about code quality. It measures what reviewers said, not whether they were right, and not whether the code was good.
  • AI-assisted reviews are invisible. Reviewer identities are essentially all human accounts — only copilot-pull-request-reviewer[bot] with 5 reviews and Copilot with 8 comments are agent identities — but a review drafted with AI help and posted under a person's account cannot be separated. Several sampled 2025–2026 bodies read as AI-assisted. This is why F3 rests on coverage and latency rather than on comment style.
  • Review rounds over-count. A reviewer leaving eight inline comments in one pass is one round; returning three times is three.
  • Pending reviews are invisible, and deleted comments leave no trace. Three of 2,235 PRs already disagree with their own comment counter by one.
  • Draft adoption is unmeasurable. draft is current state, so a PR opened as a draft and later marked ready is indistinguishable from one never drafted. 10 of the 52 currently-open PRs are in draft, and that is all this supports saying.
  • Categories are lower bounds. Treat every percentage as “at least this often”.
